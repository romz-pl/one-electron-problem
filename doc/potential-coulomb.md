# Evaluation of Eigenvalues of the Schrödinger Equation with Coulomb Potential: A Review

## 1. Introduction

The time-independent Schrödinger equation with a Coulomb potential,

$$
\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2 - \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\right]\psi(\mathbf{r}) = E\,\psi(\mathbf{r}),
$$

is the archetypal exactly solvable problem of quantum mechanics: it underlies the hydrogen atom, hydrogenic ions, muonic atoms, Rydberg states, and — in modified form — quarkonium spectroscopy and screened/confined atomic systems. In atomic units ($\hbar = m_e = e = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1$), separating variables in spherical coordinates $\psi = \dfrac{u(r)}{r}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)$ reduces the problem to the radial equation

$$
-\frac{1}{2}u''(r) + \left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^2} - \frac{Z}{r}\right]u(r) = E\,u(r), \qquad u(0)=0,
$$

whose bound-state spectrum is famously given in closed form by

$$
E_n = -\frac{Z^2}{2n^2}, \qquad n = 1,2,3,\dots,\quad \ell = 0,1,\dots,n-1.
$$

Because the pure Coulomb problem is exactly solvable (via separation in spherical or parabolic coordinates, the factorization/ladder-operator method, or the hidden $SO(4)$ dynamical symmetry), it is rarely the eigenvalues of the *bare* potential that occupy the numerical-methods literature. Instead, the Coulomb problem serves two roles in the eigenvalue-evaluation literature reviewed here:

1. **A benchmark.** Because $E_n=-Z^2/2n^2$ is known exactly, the pure Coulomb problem is the standard test case against which every new numerical or semi-analytical eigenvalue method — finite element, finite difference, spectral/basis-set, shooting, matrix, or perturbative — is validated before being applied to non-solvable potentials.
2. **A component of composite potentials.** Most of the active literature concerns Coulomb-plus-X potentials (linear, harmonic, Yukawa/screened, Hulthén, Cornell, ring-shaped, non-central, or singular perturbations) for which the added term destroys exact solvability and eigenvalues must be obtained numerically or by approximate analytic techniques, then checked against the $Z^2/2n^2$ limit.

This review surveys (i) the analytic structure of the problem, (ii) the major families of numerical/semi-analytical eigenvalue methods applied to it, (iii) known subtleties (Coulomb singularity, continuum, relativistic corrections), and (iv) representative applications, closing with a curated bibliography.

## 2. Analytic Structure of the Problem

### 2.1 Exact solvability

The radial equation for $V(r) = -Z/r$ can be solved by:

- **Series/Frobenius method** — the wavefunction is a confluent hypergeometric (Laguerre) function; polynomial truncation of the series yields the quantization condition $E_n=-Z^2/(2n^2)$.
- **Factorization / SUSY-QM ladder operators** — Schrödinger's and Infeld–Hull's factorization method generates the spectrum algebraically without solving a differential equation directly.
- **Parabolic coordinates** — separates the Stark-perturbed hydrogen problem and is the natural coordinate system for treating an external electric field.
- **Dynamical symmetry** — the Coulomb problem possesses a hidden $SO(4)$ (bound states) / $SO(3,1)$ (continuum) symmetry generated by the angular momentum and Runge–Lenz vector, which fixes the $n^2$-fold degeneracy and provides a purely group-theoretic derivation of the spectrum (Pauli's original 1926 solution predates Schrödinger's wave-mechanical one).
- **Momentum-space (Fock) representation** — Fock's stereographic projection maps the momentum-space Schrödinger equation onto the integral equation for spherical harmonics on $S^3$, again yielding $E_n=-Z^2/2n^2$ and clarifying the $O(4)$ symmetry.

### 2.2 The Coulomb singularity as a numerical difficulty

Even though the pure problem is solvable, the $1/r$ singularity at the origin is the single greatest source of numerical difficulty when the Coulomb term appears inside a non-solvable composite potential or when discretizing on a uniform grid:

- Finite-difference and finite-element schemes on a uniform mesh converge slowly near $r=0$ unless the mesh is graded, a logarithmic/exponential coordinate transformation is used, or basis functions with the correct Coulombic cusp ($\partial\psi/\partial r|_{r\to 0} \propto -Z\psi(0)$, the Kato cusp condition) are chosen.
- In momentum space, the Coulomb potential's Fourier transform is itself singular ($\propto 1/k^2$), requiring specialized (e.g., Landé-subtracted or hypersingular) quadrature rather than naive quadrature.
- Any variational/basis-set method (Gaussian-type orbitals, harmonic-oscillator bases, B-splines) converges only algebraically, not exponentially, unless the basis explicitly incorporates the correct short-range cusp and long-range exponential decay.

## 3. Numerical and Semi-Analytical Methods for Eigenvalue Evaluation

### 3.1 Finite-difference methods (FDM)

The radial equation is discretized on a grid $r_j = j\Delta$, producing a tridiagonal (nearest-neighbor) Hamiltonian matrix whose eigenvalues are obtained by standard linear-algebra routines. This is the pedagogically standard approach and remains competitive for one-dimensional radial problems. Key practical issues: grid spacing $\Delta$ must be small enough to resolve the cusp at $r=0$ and the exponential tail at large $r$; non-uniform (e.g., logarithmically spaced) grids substantially improve accuracy for a fixed number of points.

### 3.2 Finite element methods (FEM)

FEM discretizes the variational (weak) form of the Schrödinger equation using piecewise polynomial basis functions on a mesh, converting the eigenvalue problem into a generalized matrix eigenvalue problem $K\mathbf{c} = E M \mathbf{c}$. Relative to FDM, FEM offers:

- Natural mesh grading/refinement near the Coulomb singularity and near nuclei in multi-center problems;
- Straightforward extension to two- and three-dimensional and even higher-dimensional (multi-electron) problems by tensorization or genuinely multi-dimensional elements;
- Rigorous error control via the variational (Rayleigh–Ritz) upper-bound property, and — in more recent work — **two-sided (upper and lower) eigenvalue bounds** via non-conforming elements such as the Crouzeix–Raviart family, which is of particular interest for the singular, sign-indefinite Coulomb Hamiltonian on unbounded domains.
- Scalability to large parallel computations for genuinely 3D electronic-structure problems (relevant, e.g., to massively parallel FEM solvers of the one-electron Schrödinger equation run on high-performance clusters).

FEM has been applied to the plain hydrogen atom (radial and full 3D), the hydrogen atom confined in a box or under external fields (Stark effect), the H$_2^+$ molecular ion, and multi-electron atoms (helium-like, lithium-like) via dimensional reduction of the many-body Schrödinger equation.

### 3.3 Basis-set / spectral (Rayleigh–Ritz, B-spline, generalized pseudospectral) methods

Expansion of the wavefunction in a finite basis (Laguerre functions, B-splines, Gaussian-type orbitals, harmonic-oscillator eigenfunctions, or Chebyshev/pseudospectral collocation points) turns the differential eigenvalue problem into an algebraic (generalized) matrix eigenvalue problem. B-spline bases are particularly favored in atomic physics because of their local support, arbitrary-order smoothness control, and ability to represent both bound and continuum (pseudo-)states on a single finite box. Generalized pseudospectral methods use non-uniform, Coulomb-adapted grids to achieve spectral (exponential) convergence for the lowest eigenvalues at modest basis size.

### 3.4 The Hill-determinant method

Wavefunction is written as a power series times the correct asymptotic factor, substituted into the Schrödinger equation to generate a recursion relation among the expansion coefficients, and the vanishing of the determinant of the resulting (infinite, truncated) matrix — the Hill determinant — furnishes the quantization condition. Historically important for Coulomb-plus-anharmonic problems but known to suffer from **slow/absent convergence for higher excited states** and can produce spurious, unphysical roots; it also does not enforce the correct boundary decay of the wavefunction automatically, a known limitation relative to later methods.

### 3.5 The Riccati–Padé method

Rewrites the Schrödinger equation as a nonlinear Riccati equation for the logarithmic derivative of the wavefunction, then constructs Padé approximants of the resulting expansion; the poles of well-chosen Padé approximants converge to the exact bound-state (and even resonance) eigenvalues. Used extensively for Coulomb-type and anharmonic-Coulombic potentials, including cases with irregular singularities.

### 3.6 Asymptotic Iteration Method (AIM)

Transforms the radial Schrödinger equation into the second-order linear ODE $y'' = \lambda_0(x) y' + s_0(x) y$ and generates the quantization condition from the "asymptotic" termination of an iterated coefficient sequence, $\delta_n = \lambda_n s_{n-1} - \lambda_{n-1} s_n = 0$. AIM reproduces the exact closed-form spectrum where the potential is exactly solvable and, for potentials that are not (e.g., Coulomb + linear + harmonic terms, i.e. Coulomb-plus-Cornell type problems), generates the eigenvalues iteratively/numerically to high precision. It has become one of the most widely used semi-analytical techniques for Coulomb-type problems over the last two decades, and is routinely benchmarked against the shifted $1/N$ expansion, moment methods, and SUSY-QM results.

### 3.7 Supersymmetric quantum mechanics (SUSY-QM) and shape invariance

Recasts the eigenvalue problem via a superpotential $W(r)$ satisfying the Riccati equation associated with factorizing the Hamiltonian, $H_- = A^\dagger A$, $H_+ = AA^\dagger$. When the partner potentials are "shape invariant," the entire spectrum follows algebraically without solving a differential equation. The Coulomb potential is the original and best-known shape-invariant example; SUSY-QM techniques have subsequently been extended to Coulomb-plus-polynomial and Coulomb-plus-ring-shaped potentials, often supplying exact results for special parameter values and reference values for the remaining, numerically-solved cases.

### 3.8 Nikiforov–Uvarov (NU) method and its parametric generalization

Reduces a generalized hypergeometric-type equation (into which the radial Schrödinger equation can be cast after a suitable coordinate/approximation transformation) to a form solvable via Rodrigues-type formulas for classical orthogonal polynomials. The Coulomb, harmonic-oscillator, Kratzer, pseudoharmonic, Hulthén, and Woods–Saxon-type potentials are all standard NU-method test cases; the widely used "parametric NU method" of Tezcan and Sever streamlines the bookkeeping. For Coulomb potentials perturbed by a centrifugal ($\ell\neq0$) term, an additional Pekeris-type or Greene–Aldrich approximation of the centrifugal barrier is typically required to preserve exact solvability, introducing a controlled additional approximation whose accuracy is then checked numerically.

### 3.9 The $1/N$ (shifted large-$N$) expansion

Treats the spatial dimensionality $N$ (or an analogous large parameter) as a formal expansion variable, with the Coulomb problem's exact solvability providing the leading (classical/mean-field) term and quantum corrections generated order by order. Long used as an independent high-accuracy benchmark against which AIM, NU, and SUSY-QM results for Coulomb-type potentials are checked.

### 3.10 Variational, perturbative, and moment methods

Direct Rayleigh–Ritz variational calculations with Coulomb-adapted trial functions, Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory (treating an added linear/harmonic/screening term as a perturbation on the exactly solvable Coulomb problem), and moment-based methods (using expectation values / sum rules to bound or estimate eigenvalues) round out the classical toolbox and are frequently used as cross-checks alongside the semi-analytical methods above.

### 3.11 Momentum-space quadrature methods

For Coulomb, linear-confinement, and Cornell (Coulomb + linear) potentials relevant to quarkonium spectroscopy, the Schrödinger equation is solved directly in momentum space as a singular/hypersingular integral equation. Specialized (Landé-subtracted) quadrature schemes handle the Coulomb kernel's $1/k^2$ singularity, achieving very high (many-digit) accuracy — a methodology developed specifically because the naive momentum-space treatment of the Coulomb term is otherwise ill-conditioned.

### 3.12 Explicit two-sided (guaranteed) eigenvalue bounds

The most recent methodological direction combines domain truncation of the (formally unbounded) Coulomb problem with a Composite Enriched Crouzeix–Raviart (CECR) finite element construction to produce **rigorous, explicitly computable lower bounds** on the eigenvalues (complementing the standard conforming-FEM upper bounds from the Rayleigh–Ritz principle). This is applied directly to $V(r) = -Z/|x|$ (hydrogen atom) and to the two-center Coulomb problem (H$_2^+$), giving certified enclosures rather than only convergent numerical estimates — of particular interest where mathematically guaranteed accuracy (rather than empirical convergence) is required.

## 4. Extensions and Complications Commonly Layered on the Coulomb Problem

- **Screened/Yukawa and exponential-cosine screened Coulomb potentials** — model electron screening in plasmas and solids; destroy exact solvability, driving most of the AIM/NU/SUSY-QM literature above.
- **Cornell and Coulomb + linear + harmonic potentials** — central to quarkonium (heavy quark–antiquark) spectroscopy in particle physics; combine a short-distance Coulombic term with a long-distance confining term.
- **Hulthén and Coulomb-plus-Hulthén / Coulomb-plus-Yukawa mixed potentials** — used as more realistic short-range nuclear/atomic models with a screened-Coulomb-like exponential form that reduces to the pure Coulomb potential in an appropriate limit.
- **Non-central and ring-shaped Coulomb-type potentials** — arise in the study of ring molecules and certain diatomic/interaction models; solved via separation into a Coulomb-type radial part plus an angular part with a modified effective angular momentum.
- **D-dimensional generalizations** — the Coulomb problem is frequently re-derived in arbitrary spatial dimension $N$ or $D$, both to test the generality of a proposed method and because the large-$D$/large-$N$ limit underlies the $1/N$ expansion technique itself.
- **Relativistic corrections (Dirac–Coulomb, Klein–Gordon–Coulomb equations)** — the radial Dirac equation with a Coulomb potential is also exactly solvable, and is a standard benchmark for relativistic finite-element and NU/AIM-type solvers; naive discretizations are notorious for producing **spurious eigenvalues** that must be identified and excluded.
- **Confinement effects** — the hydrogen atom confined in an impenetrable or soft (e.g., inverted-Gaussian) spherical box, and hydrogen in a uniform external electric field (linear Stark effect), are classic non-perturbative modifications solved primarily by FEM/FDM since exact solvability is lost (or, for the linear Stark effect in parabolic coordinates, only approximately preserved).
- **Energy-dependent Coulomb-type potentials** — a more recent generalization in which the potential itself depends on the sought eigenvalue $E$, requiring a self-consistent or iterative numerical solution; used to model certain confined and heavy-quark systems.

## 5. Summary Comparison of Method Families

| Method family | Exactness for pure Coulomb | Typical use | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Series/factorization/SUSY-QM (exact) | Exact, closed form | Reference / benchmark | Only applies as-is to exactly solvable cases |
| Finite difference | Numerically exact in the continuum limit | Simple 1D radial problems, teaching | Slow convergence near $r=0$ without grid grading |
| Finite element (incl. two-sided/CECR) | Numerically exact in the limit; can give certified bounds | 1D–3D, multi-center, HPC-scale problems | Mesh generation/refinement overhead; singularity handling |
| Basis-set/spectral (B-spline, pseudospectral) | Near-exact with adapted basis | High-precision atomic structure | Basis must respect cusp & decay for fast convergence |
| Hill determinant | Approximate | Historic Coulomb + anharmonic problems | Poor convergence for excited states; spurious roots |
| Riccati–Padé | Approximate, often very accurate | Coulomb + anharmonic, resonances | Convergence not always guaranteed |
| AIM | Exact where solvable; iterative otherwise | Coulomb-plus-X potentials broadly | Iteration truncation choice affects accuracy |
| Nikiforov–Uvarov (parametric) | Exact for solvable cases | Wide range of Coulomb-type potentials | Non-zero $\ell$ needs centrifugal-term approximation |
| Shifted $1/N$ expansion | Asymptotically exact (large $N$) | Cross-check / benchmark | Series must be truncated; less accurate at low $N$ |
| Momentum-space quadrature | High precision (many digits) | Cornell/quarkonium spectroscopy | Requires specialized singular-kernel quadrature |

## 6. Concluding Remarks

The pure Coulomb Schrödinger eigenvalue problem, $E_n = -Z^2/2n^2$, has been solved exactly since the earliest days of quantum mechanics through several independent routes (Frobenius series, factorization/ladder operators, parabolic separation, and $SO(4)$ dynamical symmetry). Its enduring role in the numerical-methods literature is twofold: as the universal benchmark against which every finite-difference, finite-element, spectral, variational, and semi-analytical (AIM, NU, SUSY-QM, $1/N$, Riccati–Padé, Hill-determinant) eigenvalue technique is first validated, and as one term in the much larger family of physically motivated Coulomb-plus-X potentials — screened, confined, ring-shaped, Cornell-type, relativistic, or dimensionally generalized — for which exact solvability is lost and accurate eigenvalue evaluation genuinely requires the numerical and semi-analytical machinery surveyed above. The most recent methodological trend, exemplified by explicit two-sided FEM eigenvalue bounds for singular Schrödinger operators, moves the field from convergent numerical estimates toward mathematically certified enclosures, directly confronting the Coulomb singularity rather than merely working around it.
