# Evaluation of Eigenvalues of the Schrödinger Equation with the Eckart Potential: A Review

## 1. Introduction

The Eckart potential is one of a small family of exponential-type interaction models — alongside the Morse, Pöschl–Teller, Hulthén, Rosen–Morse, Woods–Saxon, and Manning–Rosen potentials — for which the time-independent Schrödinger equation can be solved in closed or near-closed form. It was introduced by Carl Eckart in 1930 as a smooth analytic barrier suitable for describing electron tunneling, in a form soluble in terms of hypergeometric functions, from which the reflection coefficient could be calculated explicitly. Since then the potential has become a standard model in two largely separate contexts: (i) *scattering/barrier-penetration problems*, where the potential appears as a smooth bell-shaped barrier separating two asymptotically free regions, and (ii) *bound-state (spectroscopic) problems*, where a related but distinct form of the potential — the "Eckart" or "Eckart–Manning–Rosen" well — is used to model the vibrational and rotational structure of diatomic molecules as an alternative to the Morse potential, with the advantage of a finite, physically-motivated behavior at short internuclear distance.

This review is organized around the *evaluation of eigenvalues* — i.e., the specific mathematical and numerical methods that have been used to obtain the discrete (bound-state) energy spectrum of the Schrödinger equation for the Eckart potential and its many generalizations, including the centrifugal (rotational) barrier, position-dependent mass, D-dimensional, relativistic (Klein–Gordon and Dirac), and PT-symmetric extensions.

## 2. Forms of the Eckart Potential

Several inequivalent parameterizations appear in the literature, and confusion between them is a common pitfall.

### 2.1 Eckart's original barrier (1930)

$$
V(x) = \frac{v_0}{\sinh^2(\alpha x)} + \frac{v_1}{\tanh(\alpha x)}, \qquad -\infty < x < \infty
$$

equivalently written as

$$
V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\left[A\,\frac{e^{(x-x_0)/l}}{1+e^{(x-x_0)/l}} + B\,\frac{e^{(x-x_0)/l}}{\left(1+e^{(x-x_0)/l}\right)^2}\right].
$$

This is a **finite, asymmetric barrier** on the whole real line, tending to different constant values as $x \to \pm\infty$. It supports scattering states rather than a discrete bound spectrum in the usual sense (the "eigenvalues" of interest here are the poles of the S-matrix / transmission amplitude, or, for the symmetric case, genuine bound states below the well's rim).

### 2.2 The "well-type" (molecular/spectroscopic) Eckart potential

$$
V(r) = D\!\left[\left(\frac{1-e^{-\alpha r}}{1-\delta e^{-\alpha r}}\right)^2 - 1\right] \quad\text{or}\quad
V(r) = -\frac{A\,e^{-\alpha r}}{1-e^{-\alpha r}} + \frac{B\,e^{-\alpha r}}{(1-e^{-\alpha r})^2},
$$

restricted to $0 \le r < \infty$. This form is a genuine potential well with a finite minimum and is the version most used in diatomic-molecule spectroscopy, since (unlike the Morse potential) it is finite at $r=0$ and can be tuned to more accurately reproduce the short-range repulsive wall.

### 2.3 The hyperbolic ("trigonometric-complexified") form

$$
V(r) = A(A-1)\,\mathrm{csch}^2 r - 2B\coth r, \qquad 0 < r < \infty, \quad A>1,\ B>A^2,
$$

used extensively in the supersymmetric quantum mechanics (SUSYQM) and shape-invariance literature, and its **PT-symmetric** counterpart obtained by $r \to ix$.

### 2.4 With the centrifugal (rotational) term

For $\ell \neq 0$ states, the radial Schrödinger equation acquires the centrifugal term $\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)/2\mu r^2$, which is **not exactly compatible** with the hypergeometric structure generated by the Eckart potential. This is the central technical obstacle addressed by essentially the entire post-1980 literature on the subject (Section 4).

## 3. The Governing Equation

The radial Schrödinger equation for a particle of reduced mass $\mu$ in the Eckart potential (well form) with angular momentum $\ell$ is

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2 u(r)}{dr^2} + \left[V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2\,\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]u(r) = E\,u(r).
$$

Introducing the variable $z = e^{-\alpha r}$ (or $x=\coth(\alpha r/2)$, depending on convention) transforms the equation into one of **hypergeometric type**,

$$
z(1-z)\,u''(z) + \left[c-(a+b+1)z\right]u'(z) - ab\,u(z) = 0,
$$

whose polynomial (Jacobi) solutions exist only for a discrete, quantized set of parameters — this quantization condition is precisely the eigenvalue equation. For $\ell=0$ (s-waves) this transformation is *exact*; the presence of the centrifugal term for $\ell \ne 0$ spoils the exact hypergeometric form, which is why approximate treatments of the centrifugal term dominate the literature.

## 4. Methods Used to Evaluate the Eigenvalues

### 4.1 Exact solution via hypergeometric/Jacobi functions ($\ell = 0$)

For s-waves, or for the pure 1D problem without a centrifugal term, the substitution above yields an exact closed-form eigenvalue spectrum. For the hyperbolic form of Section 2.3:

$$
E_n = -(A+n)^2 - \frac{B^2}{(A+n)^2}, \qquad n = 0,1,2,\dots,n_{\max},
$$

with $n_{\max}$ fixed by the requirement that only a finite number of bound states exist below the continuum threshold. The associated eigenfunctions are expressed through **Jacobi polynomials** $P_n^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)$ with $x = \coth(\alpha r)$. This is the historically first and mathematically rigorous route to the spectrum, but it is limited to zero angular momentum in three dimensions (or to the strictly one-dimensional problem).

### 4.2 Approximation of the centrifugal term

Since $1/r^2$ is not compatible with the natural variable $z=e^{-\alpha r}$, several approximations have been proposed to recast $1/r^2$ in a form that preserves solvability:

- **Greene–Aldrich approximation**: $\dfrac{1}{r^2} \approx \dfrac{\alpha^2 e^{-\alpha r}}{(1-e^{-\alpha r})^2}$, valid for $\alpha r \ll 1$ (short-range/small screening-parameter regime). This is the most widely used approximation in the Eckart-potential literature.
- **Pekeris-type approximation**, originally devised for the Morse potential and adapted to Eckart-type potentials, expands $1/r^2$ about the potential minimum.
- **Improved approximation schemes** (e.g., those tuned to match both the small- and large-$r$ limits, or D-dimensional improved schemes) have been proposed to extend the accuracy of the approximation to a wider range of the screening parameter and to higher angular momentum $\ell$.

Once such an approximation is substituted, the deformed radial equation again becomes exactly solvable by hypergeometric methods, yielding an approximate closed-form eigenvalue expression valid for **arbitrary** $n$ and $\ell$.

### 4.3 Nikiforov–Uvarov (NU) method

The NU method reduces a second-order linear ODE of generalized hypergeometric type,

$$
u'' + \frac{\tilde\tau(z)}{\sigma(z)}u' + \frac{\tilde\sigma(z)}{\sigma^2(z)}u = 0,
$$

to a form solvable via Rodrigues-type formulas, producing the eigenvalue equation algebraically without needing to guess an ansatz for the wavefunction. It has become the dominant technique for the Eckart potential (with centrifugal term, Greene–Aldrich or Pekeris approximation) because of its systematic, "parametric" bookkeeping. A widely used variant, the **Nikiforov–Uvarov Functional Analysis (NUFA)** method, streamlines the algebra further via pre-tabulated parametric formulas and has been applied to the Eckart potential and combinations such as Eckart–Hellmann and Eckart–inversely-quadratic-Yukawa potentials.

### 4.4 Supersymmetric quantum mechanics (SUSYQM) and shape invariance

The Eckart potential belongs to the classic list of *shape-invariant* potentials (together with the Coulomb, harmonic oscillator, Morse, and Pöschl–Teller potentials) discovered by Gendenshtein. In this approach:

1. The ground-state wavefunction is used to construct a superpotential $W(r)$ via $W = -\psi_0'/\psi_0$.
2. Supersymmetric partner potentials $V_{\pm} = W^2 \pm W'$ are formed.
3. If $V_+(r;a_0)$ and $V_-(r;a_0) = V_+(r;a_1) + R(a_0)$ (shape invariance with parameter shift $a_0 \to a_1$), the entire spectrum follows algebraically from summing the "remainders" $R(a_k)$,

$$
E_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} R(a_k).
$$

This method yields the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions without directly solving the differential equation, and it has been combined with the **SUSY WKB** approximation and with **shape-invariance under parameter scaling** to treat the Eckart potential including a spin–orbit or centrifugal term. Rational (Darboux–Crum) extensions of the shape-invariant Eckart potential have also been constructed, yielding isospectral or nearly-isospectral deformed partners with exceptional-orthogonal-polynomial eigenfunctions.

### 4.5 Asymptotic Iteration Method (AIM)

AIM converts the Schrödinger equation, after a suitable substitution, into the form $u'' = \lambda_0(x)u' + s_0(x)u$, then generates a sequence $\lambda_k, s_k$ via differentiation; the quantization condition is the "termination" condition $s_k\lambda_{k-1} - s_{k-1}\lambda_k = 0$. AIM has been applied both analytically (obtaining closed-form eigenvalue expressions after series truncation) and numerically (iterating to convergence) for the Eckart potential and its PT-symmetric version, generally in combination with the Greene–Aldrich centrifugal approximation, and has also been used for a "multiparameter" potential of which the Eckart potential is a special case.

### 4.6 Exact/semi-numerical methods for the general $\ell$ problem

For situations where an approximation to the centrifugal term is undesirable, several exact-numerical strategies have been used:

- **Confluent Heun function** representations of the exact radial equation (retaining the true $1/r^2$ term), with eigenvalues extracted from the vanishing of a Wronskian-type determinant/quantization condition — used, e.g., for combined Woods–Saxon plus Eckart potentials.
- **Direct numerical integration / finite-difference or shooting methods**, used as a benchmark against which the various analytic approximations (NU, AIM, SUSYQM) are validated. Papers on the "Hua plus modified Eckart potential" and similar composite potentials explicitly compare exact-analytic (in the solvable sub-case) against finite-difference numerical eigenvalues.
- **Variational/R-matrix methods**, more common on the scattering side, used to extract resonance positions (which correspond to quasi-bound "eigenvalues" embedded in or near the continuum) for the barrier form of the potential.

### 4.7 Semiclassical (WKB) evaluation

The WKB quantization condition can be applied directly to the Eckart potential; it is exactly solvable in closed form because the corresponding classical turning-point integral is elementary. It is well documented, however, that the **ordinary WKB result does not reproduce the exact quantum eigenvalues** obtained from the hypergeometric solution — a longstanding example (alongside the Morse and Pöschl–Teller potentials) used to illustrate the limitations of the leading-order WKB approximation and to test **Langer-corrected** or higher-order WKB/perturbative refinements.

### 4.8 Position-dependent mass (PDM), higher dimensions, and relativistic wave equations

The eigenvalue problem has been generalized along several independent axes, each requiring its own eigenvalue-extraction technique built on the tools above:

- **Position-dependent effective mass** Schrödinger equation with the Eckart-family potential, solved via a point-canonical (coordinate) transformation that maps the PDM equation onto a standard constant-mass hypergeometric equation.
- ***D*-dimensional** generalizations, where the earlier NU/AIM machinery is repeated with the dimension $D$ entering the effective centrifugal term $\ell(\ell+1) \to (\ell + (D-3)/2)(\ell+(D-1)/2)$.
- **Klein–Gordon equation** with equal scalar and vector Eckart (and Eckart–Rosen–Morse) potentials, solved via NU or AIM, including PT-symmetric versions where non-Hermitian potentials nonetheless yield real eigenvalues by virtue of PT symmetry.
- **Dirac equation** with the Eckart potential (including spin–orbit/pseudospin coupling terms), solved via SUSY-WKB and functional-analysis (NUFA-type) methods, relevant to relativistic nuclear mean-field-type problems.

### 4.9 Combined/hybrid potentials

Because the Eckart potential alone does not always reproduce experimental molecular spectra with sufficient accuracy, a large sub-literature evaluates eigenvalues for **sums of Eckart with other exactly/quasi-exactly solvable potentials**, most commonly:

- Eckart + Hellmann potential,
- Eckart + inversely-quadratic Yukawa potential,
- Eckart + Manning–Rosen potential,
- Deformed/generalized Eckart + Deng–Fan potential (relativistic, Klein–Gordon),
- Möbius-square + Eckart potential (studied in noncommutative phase space),
- Woods–Saxon + Eckart potential (toroidal coordinates).

These are solved with the same toolkit (NU/NUFA, SUSYQM, AIM) applied to the sum potential, generally still requiring a Greene–Aldrich- or Pekeris-type centrifugal approximation.

## 5. Representative Exact Result (1D / s-wave Case)

For the potential $V(x) = v_0/\sinh^2(\alpha x) + v_1/\tanh(\alpha x)$, the exact bound-state spectrum obtained from the hypergeometric solution is

$$
E_n = -\frac{2\mu v_1^2}{\alpha^2\hbar^2\left(\sqrt{1+8\mu v_0/\alpha^2\hbar^2}+2n+1\right)^2} \;-\; \frac{\alpha^2\hbar^2\left(\sqrt{1+8\mu v_0/\alpha^2\hbar^2}+2n+1\right)^2}{8\mu},
$$

which is frequently used as the benchmark against which WKB, NU, AIM, and SUSYQM results (and their various centrifugal-term approximations) are validated in the $\ell=0$ / one-dimensional limit.

## 6. Applications

- **Molecular spectroscopy**: vibrational–rotational energy levels of diatomic molecules (e.g. CO, HCl, LiH, CuLi, TiH, VH, TiC, NiC, ScF, CrH, SiF⁺), where Eckart or Eckart-combination potentials are fitted to spectroscopic (Rydberg–Klein–Rees, RKR) data and compared against Morse-potential predictions.
- **Chemical reaction-rate theory**: the original barrier form of the potential is the standard analytic model for computing quantum-mechanical **tunneling corrections** to transition-state-theory rate constants (Eckart tunneling correction), still in routine use in astrochemistry and combustion kinetics for barrier-crossing reaction networks.
- **Thermodynamic properties**: partition functions and derived quantities (mean energy, entropy, free energy, specific heat) for a gas of particles bound by the Eckart or Eckart-combination potential.
- **Information-theoretic quantities**: Shannon and Rényi entropies, Fisher information, and expectation values computed from Eckart-potential eigenfunctions.
- **Nuclear and particle physics**: Dirac/Klein–Gordon treatments of Eckart-type potentials for meson/quarkonium mass spectra and pseudospin-symmetry studies.
- **Analogue/toy models**: black-hole thermodynamic-stability analyses have used the Eckart-potential spectrum as an input via the Rényi entropy of the associated wavefunctions.
- **Quantum tunneling and scattering theory**: reflection/transmission coefficients through smooth (Eckart) barriers, used both as an analytically tractable proxy for more realistic barriers (e.g. Gaussian barriers in cold-atom Josephson-junction experiments) and as a pedagogical alternative to the rectangular barrier.

## 9. Concluding Remarks

The Eckart potential occupies a distinctive niche among exactly solvable quantum models: it is one of the few potentials with genuine physical motivation on both sides of the bound-state/scattering divide — as a molecular potential well and as a tunneling barrier. Its eigenvalue problem is *exactly* solvable only in the ℓ = 0 (or strictly 1D) case; the practically important arbitrary-ℓ, 3D (or D-dimensional) problem requires an approximation of the centrifugal term, almost universally the Greene–Aldrich or a Pekeris-type substitution. On top of this common foundation, four largely equivalent but methodologically distinct machineries — the Nikiforov–Uvarov method (and its NUFA variant), supersymmetric shape-invariance, the asymptotic iteration method, and direct hypergeometric/Jacobi-polynomial analysis — have each been used repeatedly, and cross-validated against exact numerical (finite-difference, shooting, or confluent Heun function) benchmarks and against WKB estimates. The resulting toolkit has since been exported wholesale to relativistic wave equations (Klein–Gordon, Dirac), position-dependent-mass systems, higher-dimensional spaces, PT-symmetric and non-Hermitian extensions, and a large family of Eckart-combination potentials tailored to specific diatomic molecules, making the "evaluation of Eckart-potential eigenvalues" a template problem for exactly- and quasi-exactly-solvable quantum mechanics more broadly.
